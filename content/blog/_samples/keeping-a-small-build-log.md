A commit tells me what changed. It rarely captures why I tried it, what surprised me, or which idea I decided to leave alone. Those details fade faster than I expect.

A build log is my attempt to keep a little of that context. Nothing elaborate: a date, a few lines, and enough detail to help my future self pick up the thread.

## Write while it is fresh

The best time to write a note is usually just after making a decision. I still remember the alternatives and the particular constraint that made one option feel right.

Waiting until a project is finished makes the story tidier than the work was. I want to preserve a few of the rough edges: the approach that almost worked and the small observation that changed the next step.

```markdown title="build-log.md"
## August 12

Tried a second toolbar for board actions.
It made the first screen harder to understand.

Kept the common action beside the board title.
Next: try the keyboard path with an empty board.
```

## Capture decisions, not everything

The log is useful when it helps me resume work. It does not need to become a transcript of the day. I usually write about something I learned, a tradeoff I made, or a question that is still open.

- What did I change my mind about?
- What was harder than I expected?
- What should I try next?

A screenshot can help when the decision was visual. A short code example can help when the surprise was technical. Most days, a paragraph is enough.

## Let a post grow from a note

Some notes stay private and practical. Others reveal a pattern after a few weeks: a question I keep asking or a mistake I keep learning from. Those are the ones I would like to turn into longer posts.

Writing publicly feels easier when I already have something specific to say. The small log gives me a place to notice it before worrying about a title.

> A few honest lines today can become a useful story later.
