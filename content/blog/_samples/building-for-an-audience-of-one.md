Most of my projects begin with a small interruption. Something I do every day takes a few more steps than it should. I notice it, work around it, and eventually open an editor.

That is a modest place to start. It is also the place I keep coming back to. Before a tool has a roadmap or an audience, it can have one useful job and one person who needs it.

## Start with the friction

The problems that interest me rarely arrive as fully formed product ideas. They look more like a note I cannot find, a weekly plan I keep rewriting, or a task that gets lost between two apps.

I try to sit with that friction before deciding what to build. Where does it happen? How often? What am I doing immediately before and after it? Sometimes the answer is a shortcut. Sometimes it is a small plugin. Occasionally it deserves an app.

> Would I still use this if nobody else ever did?

That question is a useful filter. It takes the launch out of the picture for a moment and brings the work back to something concrete: making an ordinary day a little better.

## Make the smallest useful version

A small first version should complete a real task. It does not need to account for every person who might eventually use it, but the path it supports should work from beginning to end.

For a weekly planning tool, that might mean choosing a priority, putting it on a day, and finding it again tomorrow. Themes, templates, and integrations can wait until that loop feels right.

- Name the recurring problem in one sentence.
- Build one complete path through it.
- Use it during actual work for a week.
- Write down the moments that still feel awkward.

I like constraints that I can feel. One screen. Ordinary files. A feature that can be explained without a diagram. They make decisions easier when every interesting possibility is competing for the same evening.

## Let use shape the work

Using something you made is a different kind of feedback. You discover the extra click when you are tired. You notice the missing keyboard shortcut when you are in a hurry. You find out whether a clever interaction is still helpful on the twentieth attempt.

I keep a short list of these observations. Not every item becomes a feature. Sometimes the right change is removing an option, choosing a better default, or making a label more honest.

> [!NOTE] A useful constraint
>
> Try improving a workflow you already repeat before inventing a new habit for your future self.

This also changes what progress looks like. A quieter interface or a more reliable save can matter more than a screenshot with three new features.

## Leave room for other people

Building for yourself has a blind spot: you already know how everything works. Sharing an early version is a good way to discover which assumptions live only in your head.

I want to stay open to that feedback without losing the original thread. A personal tool can grow into something useful to other people. It can also remain small. Both outcomes leave me with something I am glad I made.

For now, that feels like a good reason to keep building.
